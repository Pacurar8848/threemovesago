Retrograde analysis: reasoning backwards in chess

Most chess thinking goes forwards: what is the best move now? Retrograde analysis goes the other way. You look at a position and ask what must already have happened.

The idea

Every chess position is the end of a story. Pieces cannot jump, pawns never move backwards, and a captured piece is gone for good. Because the rules are so strict, a position often contains enough evidence to rebuild part of the game that produced it. Working that out is called retrograde analysis, or retro analysis for short.

It is a long-standing branch of chess problem composition. It reached a wide audience through the logician Raymond Smullyan, whose book The Chess Mysteries of Sherlock Holmes turns retro problems into detective stories.

The clues a retro solver looks for

  • Material. Count the pieces. Every missing piece was captured, and every capture was made by a piece of the other colour.
  • Pawn files. A pawn can only change file by capturing. A pawn on the wrong file, or two pawns on the same file, proves a capture happened.
  • Pieces that cannot have left. A bishop behind its own unmoved pawns has never moved. A rook in the corner with its king beside it may never have moved either.
  • Kings. A king away from its home square must have walked there, one square per move, and only through squares that were not attacked at the time.
  • Checks. Only one side can be in check, and a check must have been given by the last move. This often fixes the order of moves.
  • Tempo. If one side needed fewer moves than it had, the extra moves were spent somewhere. A piece that went out and came back is a classic answer.

A small example

White is missing the e-pawn and has a knight on c3. Black is missing the d-pawn, and at first sight Black’s other pieces have not moved at all. But Black played three moves, so something did move. The d-pawn must have gone to d5, and the white e-pawn must have taken it there. Then who took the white pawn on d5? Only the queen could reach it, and it has since gone home.

1. e4 d5 2. exd5 Qxd5 3. Nc3 Qd8

The queen on d8 looks as if it never moved, but it left, captured, and returned. That is the core trick of retro chess: the position hides moves that left no visible trace.
